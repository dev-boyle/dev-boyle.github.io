Overview

This is a two-piece program with a poem in the middle, and the two pieces sit 130 years apart: Dvořák's trio dates from 1891, Tucker's Melting from the pandemic winter of 2020–21. The title Chatter gave the morning, "Transformations," fits both. Tucker's piece is about a slow thaw, one state turning into another. Dvořák's trio is built from six movements that each pivot, sometimes in a single bar, from lament to dance.

The arc moves from a quiet, inward elegy for a hard stretch of time, through spoken word and a pause for silence, into one of the most beloved works in the piano trio literature. It also runs from a piece written for the clarinet-violin-cello-piano lineup to one written for a trio without clarinet. Dvořák's Op. 90 is scored for piano, violin and cello, so James Shields presumably sits that one out and the other three players carry the second half.

The listing doesn't mention any cuts, so I've assumed the full six movements of the Dvořák. About 30 minutes of trio plus Tucker's 7½-minute piece, the poem and the silence should fit Chatter's roughly one-hour format.

Akshaya Tucker — Melting (2021)

▶ Listen (Soli Ensemble, SoundCloud)▶ Tucker's work page

Tucker wrote Melting in the winter of 2020–21, deep in the pandemic, with a blunt goal: to write the saddest music she could think of. Her program note pictures a slow thaw, the earth letting go of winter. It also describes a spring that arrives too soon and too violently, erratic and out of step with the thaw underneath it. Behind the image is the experience of lockdown: long months indoors with our own thoughts, old hurts finally coming into the light, some acknowledged and released, others lingering. The note ends on something like a mantra about falling and melting, letting every hurt drop away and heal. She calls the whole thing one long pandemic exhale.

The piece is for clarinet, violin, cello and piano, the same four-instrument combination as Messiaen's Quartet for the End of Time, which is also the lineup of San Antonio's SOLI Chamber Ensemble. Her note thanks SOLI and the composer Alexandra Gardner for their part in its creation, and the Soli Ensemble's recording, posted to Tucker's SoundCloud in June 2021, is the one linked above. It has since been heard at Interlochen, where Tucker teaches composition, and has been programmed by Los Angeles's Brightwork Ensemble.

Tucker is based in Los Angeles. She trained as a cellist and as an Odissi dancer from a young age, and her music often draws on South Asian music and dance traditions, with themes of care, presence and the climate crisis. In 2026 she is composer-in-residence at both ChatterPDX and ChatterABQ, and in 2025 she held the same role at the Willamette Valley Chamber Music Festival. Her string quartet Hollow Flame appears on Brooklyn Rider's recent album The Four Elements.

Listen for

  • A very slow, spacious unfolding. Her note imagines a gradual thaw, so listen for how the sound loosens and eases rather than building to a conventional climax.
  • The tension her note describes between "slow" and "erratic." Keep an ear out for abrupt or disruptive gestures set against the stretched-out backdrop.
  • Sinking or dissolving shapes. Her note keeps returning to falling and melting, so see whether the four instruments seem to drift downward or fade into each other.
  • Sounds that hang in the air or keep returning. Lingering is one of the note's central images.
  • The color of the lineup: the clarinet's breath-like tone against the strings and piano.

I haven't been able to listen to the recording, so these listen-fors come from Tucker's own program note and her general style as described in her bios. Treat them as prompts, not a map, and see which ones hold up when you hear the SoundCloud track.

Spoken Word — Alex Dang

Placed mid-program, between the Tucker and the Dvořák, and followed by a short stretch of silence. After the held, inward mood of Melting, this is the hinge of the morning: a change of voice and energy before the trio's mood swings begin.

About Alex Dang

Born and raised in Portland, Oregon, Alex Dang started performing poetry at 17 and hasn't slowed down since. He was on the Portland Poetry Slam nationals team in 2013, 2014, 2015 and 2016, making him the only Asian-American poet in Portland's history to be on the team four years in a row. Alex earned his way to become the Eugene Poetry Grand Slam Champion in 2014, 2015 and again in 2017. He has been a TEDx speaker for both the University of Oregon and Reno, Nevada. His work has been featured on HuffingtonPost, UpWorthy and EverydayFeminism, and has been viewed over 2 million times on YouTube. Alex has performed in over 50 cities, 30 states and 7 countries, and wants to know what your favorite food is.

Antonín Dvořák — Piano Trio No. 4 in E minor, "Dumky," Op. 90, B. 166 (1891)

▶ Listen (Tempest Trio, Apple Music Classical)▶ Joachim Trio (Apple Music Classical)

Dvořák started this trio in November 1890, right after finishing his Requiem, and had it done by 12 February 1891, in under three months. He described it to a friend as a little piece for violin, cello and piano that would be both happy and sad: a melancholy song in places, a merry dance in others, and lighter and more popular in style than his weightier chamber music. It was premiered in Prague on 11 April 1891 at a gala evening in his honor, held just after he had been awarded an honorary degree by the city's university. Dvořák played the piano himself, with Ferdinand Lachner on violin and Hanuš Wihan on cello.

The title comes from the dumka (plural dumky), a Slavic folk form with Ukrainian roots. The word is related to a Ukrainian term meaning thought or reflection, and the traditional dumka is slow and meditative. Dvořák's version adds a second ingredient: each dumka swings between a wistful, sorrowful passage and a wildly rhythmic one. He had used that idea in movements of other works, including a string sextet, a piano quintet and the Slavonic Dances. Here he made it the entire structure, six dumky in a row in place of the usual sonata-form first movement. That is why the title is plural.

The trio was an immediate favorite, and Dvořák kept playing it. Documentation shows he performed it 44 times, mostly on a long farewell tour of Czech and Moravian towns in spring 1892, just before he left for the United States. Shortly after Dvořák arrived in America, the cellist Victor Herbert, soon to be better known as a composer for the American musical theater, was among the first to read it through with him. The score was published in Berlin by Simrock in 1894, with his friend Johannes Brahms proofreading while Dvořák was still in America. Dvořák himself admitted it was tricky to play, and the cello in particular has to carry a lot of the dumka themes.

Movement Breakdown

Six movements, each a dumka and each in a different key. The Dvořák archive notes that the first three are marked to follow one another without a pause, so you may hear them as one long span. Some commentators see a loose four-part shape hidden in the six: the first three together as an opening, the fourth as a slow movement, the fifth as a scherzo, the sixth as a finale.

  • I. Lento maestoso — Allegro quasi doppio movimento (E minor): Built on a single theme that keeps changing character: softly melancholic, passionate, and then suddenly sprightly.
  • II. Poco adagio — Vivace non troppo (C-sharp minor): Two themes and the widest mood contrast in the work, with a quiet lament set against stirring rhythms that Dvořák drives toward something like rapture.
  • III. Andante — Vivace non troppo (A major): The brightest of the first three, again built on one richly varied theme, and the last of the three that run together.
  • IV. Andante moderato (quasi tempo di marcia) — Allegretto scherzando (D minor): A march-like main theme returns several times in a free rondo, and this dumka does the job of a pensive slow movement.
  • V. Allegro (E-flat major): The only movement marked fast and the liveliest of the six, an energetic, contrapuntal scherzo with a little melancholy still showing through.
  • VI. Lento maestoso — Vivace (C minor): Two contrasting themes that sum up the whole cycle and finish with a flourish.

Listen for

  • The whiplash inside each movement: a slow, aching passage that suddenly gives way to a fast dance, then back again. The second movement has the starkest version.
  • The joins between the first three movements. They run straight into each other, so the end of one lament can become the start of the next.
  • The cello's role. It often states the dumka themes, and it's worth watching how the violin and piano answer.
  • The march of the fourth movement and how its theme keeps coming back, each time a little differently dressed.
  • The quick, bright fifth movement, then the way the sixth brings back the opening's slow-then-fast pattern for the close.

Musical Connections

Thaw and mood swing

Both works are about change of state. Tucker's thaw is slow, but her note says the spring arrives erratically, so even a gradual piece is full of disruption. Dvořák's trio is nearly the reverse: its mood changes are sudden, but the whole thing is held together by one idea repeated six times.

Grief and release

Tucker set out to write the saddest music she could imagine, and her piece ends up being about letting hurt go. The dumka is often described as melancholy, but Dvořák's version never stays sad for long, since sorrow always gets a dance as an answer. That is one way to hear the two pieces as an answer to each other.

Two ways of working with a tradition

Tucker's catalog draws broadly on South Asian music and dance traditions, though her note on Melting doesn't say the piece itself does. Dvořák took a Slavic folk form and made it the structure of a whole work. In both cases a vernacular idea gets reshaped into concert-hall chamber music.

Final Thoughts

This is a morning about music that changes from one thing into another: ice becoming water in Tucker's quiet pandemic elegy, a poem and a pause that clear the air, and then Dvořák's six dumky, where grief and a good dance keep trading places until the last flourish. It starts with something slowly letting go and ends with a trio that sounds as if it has decided to keep going.
